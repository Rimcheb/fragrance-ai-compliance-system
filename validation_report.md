## 1 & 2. Generalisation and baselines

Two cross-validation schemes over the same molecules:

- **Random** — repeated stratified 5-fold, 6 repeats (30 fits). This is the scheme the original 94% came from, and it lets near-identical analogues sit on both sides of the split.
- **Scaffold** — 5-fold `GroupKFold` on Bemis-Murcko scaffolds, so an entire structural family is held out at once. This asks whether the model generalises to chemistry it has not seen.

### Reading the table

- Fingerprints under a **random** split: 94.5%. This reproduces the original headline figure and carries the same flaw.
- Fingerprints under a **scaffold** split: 71.3% — a drop of **23.2 percentage points**. That gap is the leakage.
- Majority-class floor under the same scaffold folds: 70.5%. The honest question is not whether the model beats zero but whether it beats this.
- **The fingerprint model is indistinguishable from the majority-class baseline under a scaffold split** (71.3% vs 70.5%, a difference of +0.8 points against a fold-to-fold spread of ±12.4 points). On held-out chemistry it shows no demonstrated advantage over always guessing the most common restriction reason. The sign of that small difference is not stable across library versions; its magnitude is.
- **Name-only model** (character n-grams on the ingredient name, no chemistry at all): 67.5% under the scaffold split, against the fingerprint model's 71.3%. The two are indistinguishable, which means whatever is being learned is as available from nomenclature as from structure — chemical names encode functional class.

Balanced accuracy and macro F1 matter more than raw accuracy here, because the classes are severely imbalanced. Note that every model's balanced accuracy sits near or below 0.35 on four classes — chance is 0.25 — which says the minority classes are barely being recovered at all.

Two structural caveats on the scaffold split itself. One Murcko scaffold accounts for 112 of the 319 molecules, so the folds are necessarily uneven in size. And the smallest class has 6 members spread across folds, so some folds contain no examples of it — sklearn's warning about predicted classes absent from the true labels comes from exactly this. Both are consequences of the dataset being small and structurally lopsided, and neither is fixable by choosing a different splitter.

## 3. The positive-unlabeled problem

The comparison set is 6271 TGSC fragrance materials, of which **3529** are not in the IFRA Category 4 list and resolve to a structure.

**These are not negatives.** A material absent from the IFRA Category 4 standard is unassessed, out of scope, or not yet reviewed — it is not certified safe. The correct framing for this data is positive-unlabeled learning: one labelled positive class and a large unlabeled pool that contains an unknown number of undiscovered positives. Treating the unlabeled pool as a negative class inflates any reported precision, because a correctly-flagged-but-unlisted material is scored as a false positive.


### Provenance control

A Random Forest given **only molecular weight, LogP and TPSA** separates IFRA-listed from TGSC-unlisted materials with ROC-AUC **0.885 ± 0.026**.

That is high. Three bulk physical properties should not identify a regulatory decision. What they are almost certainly identifying is *which dataset a molecule came from* — the two sources differ in how they were compiled and in what kinds of material they contain. Any classifier trained to separate these two pools risks learning provenance rather than toxicology, and a strong restricted-vs-unregulated score should not be read as a safety signal.

## 4. Applicability domain

The OECD principles for QSAR validation require a model to declare the chemical space in which its predictions are meaningful. A model asked about a molecule unlike anything it trained on should abstain, not guess confidently.

Nearest-neighbour Tanimoto similarity **within** the training set (leave-one-out): mean 0.932, median 1.000, sd 0.185.
Applicability-domain threshold (mean − 3 sd): **0.376**. A query molecule whose nearest training neighbour is below this is outside the domain.

Of the **69** watchlist candidates, **69** fall inside the applicability domain and **0** fall outside.

All of them are inside — which is expected and *not* reassuring on its own: the watchlist was selected by requiring ≥85% similarity to a restricted molecule, so it is inside the domain by construction. The applicability domain check therefore adds nothing for this particular list. It matters for the general case, where the model is asked about an arbitrary molecule; the honest statement is that this filter constrains future queries, not this watchlist.

Reported structural similarity of the watchlist to its nearest restricted neighbour: min 85.0%, median 100.0%, max 100.0%.

**A median of 100% is the finding here.** 50 of the 69 candidates (72%) have a fingerprint *identical* to a restricted molecule. At Tanimoto 1.0 the candidate is not a structural neighbour of a regulated material — within the resolution of a 2048-bit Morgan fingerprint it is that material, listed in TGSC under a different name or registry number. Section 6 confirms this against names and canonical SMILES.

## 5. Does structural similarity predict shared restriction reason?

The watchlist rests on an unstated premise: molecules that look alike are regulated alike. That premise is testable inside the labelled data, where both the structures and the reasons are known.

| Tanimoto band | pairs | share sharing a restriction reason |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 0.00 – 0.30 | 48974 | 57.2% |
| 0.30 – 0.50 | 1214 | 65.9% |
| 0.50 – 0.70 | 315 | 79.4% |
| 0.70 – 0.85 | 28 | 64.3% |
| 0.85 – 1.00 | 33 | 84.8% |
| 1.00 – 1.00 | 157 | 97.5% |

Baseline agreement between two molecules picked at random: **56.8%** (driven by the dominant class).

The last two rows are the ones that matter, and separating them changes the conclusion. Pairs with Tanimoto ≈ 1.0 are the *same molecule* under two names — of course they share a reason, and counting them as evidence that similarity predicts regulation is circular.

- Identical structures (T ≈ 1.0): 157 pairs, 97.5% agree. Uninformative by construction.
- **Genuinely similar but distinct molecules (0.85 ≤ T < 1.0): 33 pairs, 84.8% agree.** Against a 56.8% random baseline, that is a lift of +28.1 points, and **15.2% of these highly similar pairs are regulated for different reasons**. Those are the activity cliffs — the direct measure of how often the watchlist's premise fails inside data where the answer is known.
  With only 33 such pairs in the whole dataset, this estimate is itself weakly supported. The dataset simply does not contain many pairs that are near-identical without being identical.

Spearman correlation between pairwise Tanimoto similarity and the absolute difference in permitted Category 4 limit: **ρ = -0.090** (p = 2.5e-57, n = 31626 pairs).
Essentially no relationship. Knowing that two materials are structurally similar tells you very little about whether they are permitted at similar concentrations — which is what a formulator would actually want from a substitution suggestion.

### Small-molecule fingerprint artefact

Tanimoto similarity over Morgan fingerprints is unreliable when one molecule is very small: a compact molecule sets few bits, and those bits are largely a subset of a larger molecule's, so the ratio is inflated without the two molecules being meaningfully alike.

| candidate | heavy atoms | on-bits | reported similarity | nearest restricted material |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| cyclopentanone | 6 | 10 | 90.9% | Cyclohexadecanone |
| octenal | 9 | 19 | 85.0% | trans-2-Heptenal |
| nonen-2-one | 10 | 21 | 86.4% | 3-Octen-2-one |
| hydroxymethyl hexyl ethyl ketone | 11 | 22 | 95.7% | Methyl lavender ketone |
| decadien-1-ol | 11 | 20 | 95.2% | 2,4-Dodecadien-1-ol, (2E, 4E) |

**cyclopentanone** (6 heavy atoms, 10 on-bits) is scored 90.9% similar to *Cyclohexadecanone*. Those two molecules are a five-membered ring and a sixteen-membered macrocycle. They share the substructure environments a simple cyclic ketone generates, and nothing else. This is the artefact, not a similarity finding, and it means the 85% cut-off needs a minimum-size guard before it is applied.
